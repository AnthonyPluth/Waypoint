Waypoint ships with five reference lists. The airports and airlines are loaded into the database when it is created (by the baseline migration or, on a first start, directly); the places list, the highways and the state lines are read from their files. They are the same for every Waypoint, and aren’t part of a [backup](/Waypoint/start/docker/#moving-your-data-from-another-machine).

## Airports

The airports (name, city, country, time zone and coordinates, by three-letter IATA code) come from [OurAirports](https://ourairports.com/data/)’ public-domain data, through the [`airportsdata`](https://pypi.org/project/airportsdata/) package, which adds each airport’s time zone. `tools/airports.py` writes `waypoint/storage/airports.tsv.gz`. It also writes `waypoint/storage/regions.tsv.gz` from the same data: the state of each US airport and the English name of each country, which name the places in a trip’s destination (country names come from the system’s ISO 3166 list, with a few shortened).

## Airlines

The airlines (name, IATA and ICAO code, country) come from [OpenFlights](https://openflights.org/data.html)’ `airlines.dat`, which is made available under the [Open Database License (ODbL) 1.0](https://opendatacommons.org/licenses/odbl/1-0/); its contents are under the [Database Contents License](https://opendatacommons.org/licenses/dbcl/1-0/). `tools/airlines.py` writes `waypoint/storage/airlines.tsv.gz`, one airline per IATA code. That derived file is itself a database made from OpenFlights’ and is kept open, in this public repository, under the same ODbL, as the licence asks. See also the `NOTICE` file.

## Places

The places list turns a booking’s address into a rough point on the map without asking any outside service ([Where a booking is on the map](/Waypoint/start/trips/#where-a-booking-is-on-the-map)). It holds the centre of each US ZIP code, of each Canadian postal-code area (the first three characters) and of each British postcode district (the part before the space), and the centre, state, country and population of each city of 15,000 people or more, for finding a city by its name. `tools/places.py` writes `waypoint/storage/places.tsv.gz` from these downloads, which are not fetched by the app:

- US ZIP codes: the Census Bureau’s [2023 Gazetteer ZIP Code Tabulation Areas](https://www.census.gov/geographies/reference-files/time-series/geo/gazetteer-files.html) file (US government data, public domain). A ZCTA is the Bureau’s approximation of a ZIP code, so a few ZIP codes (some PO boxes and single-building codes) have none.
- Canadian and British postal codes, and the cities: [GeoNames](https://www.geonames.org/) (`GB.zip`, `CA.zip` and `cities15000.zip` from its download server), licensed under [Creative Commons Attribution 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). The derived file keeps GeoNames’ names and coordinates, rounded to four decimals, with the British and Canadian codes averaged where GeoNames lists several places for one code, and is credited in the `NOTICE` file.

## Highways

The highways let a road trip’s route follow the real highway network instead of a curve ([Replaying a past trip’s route](/Waypoint/start/trips/#replaying-a-past-trips-route)), without asking any outside service or fetching map tiles. The list is the Interstate Highway System, US highways and state routes as the Federal Highway Administration (US Department of Transportation) publishes it in its National Highway System shapefile, version 2025-08-08, from the [NHS Maps](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/planning/national_highway_system/nhs_maps/) page (`nhs_2025-08-08.zip`, about 110 MB, not fetched by the app). It is US government data, and its metadata says the database or any portion of it “can be freely distributed as long as this metadata entry is included”, which the `NOTICE` file does. Three kinds of feature are kept: those signed as an Interstate (`SIGNT1` = `I`) and classed as one (`FCLASS` = 1), 73,431 of the file’s 492,005 features; and those signed as a US route (`U`) or a state route (`S`) with a plain route number and classed as a freeway, expressway or principal arterial (`FCLASS` = 1, 2 or 3), 287,069 more line pieces (156,339 US and 130,730 state). Alternate, business, bypass and other lettered routes, proposed routes, county routes, and anything unsigned or classed as a minor arterial or lower are left out.

`tools/roads.py` writes `waypoint/storage/roads.tsv.gz` (about 2.2 MB) from that download; run it with the path of the zip and commit the result. Its steps are the same every time, so the output is reproducible:

- Each route’s name comes from the sign and, for a state route, the state the feature is in (`I80` becomes `I-80`, `IH1` becomes `I-H1`, `U20` becomes `US-20`, and `S55` in Minnesota becomes `MN-55`).
- Pieces that meet are one junction: a piece’s end joins the other pieces of the same route that end within 0.0005 degrees (about 50 metres), and the nearest point of any other route within 0.004 degrees (about 400 metres), which is how two routes that cross at an interchange get a junction; a route passing through a junction is cut there.
- Pieces that only join each other, with nothing else at the junction, are merged into one.
- Each piece is simplified to within 0.001 degrees (about 100 metres) and its coordinates are rounded to four decimals, written as the first point and the steps from each point to the next.

The file has one row per piece: the route, its two junction numbers and its points. The app loads it once, the first time a trip’s map needs it, and keeps it in memory; finding a route is a shortest-path search over about 146,000 pieces that takes a fraction of a second (the first search after the app starts also loads the file, which takes a couple of seconds). The search counts a mile on a US highway as 1.35 miles and a mile on a state route as 1.7, so a drive stays on an interstate unless another road is much shorter. What the trip map receives is only the roads: the points and names of the highways a drive follows and of the ones near it, interstates first and within a fixed number of points (coarsened again to about a kilometre), never anything about who travels or what was booked. Because the network is coarse and leaves out minor and unsigned roads, a route can be a few miles off the road, can skip a short link that is neither an interstate, a US highway nor a numbered state route, and does not know about closures, tolls or traffic. The pieces form separate networks that don’t join (the mainland, Alaska, Hawaii, Puerto Rico and a few gaps in the data); a network of less than 50 miles is ignored, a drive is routed over one network, and a drive between two of them keeps its curve. A drive back over ground a trip already covered doesn’t search the network again: it reuses the points of the earlier drive’s line (reversed, or the stretch between the two places), so the trip map receives nothing new for it.

## State lines

The state lines are the faint dashed borders between US states that the route replay draws behind the route and the highways once the map is close enough to read them ([Replaying a past trip’s route](/Waypoint/start/trips/#replaying-a-past-trips-route)), without asking any outside service or fetching map tiles. They come from the US Census Bureau’s 2024 Cartographic Boundary File for States and Equivalent Entities at 1:5,000,000 (`cb_2024_us_state_5m.zip`, about 1.1 MB, from the [Cartographic Boundary Files](https://www.census.gov/geographies/mapping-files/time-series/geo/cartographic-boundary.html) page, not fetched by the app). It is US government data, and its metadata gives “Access constraints: None”: it is in the public domain, with no licence terms or required credit.

`tools/states.py` writes `waypoint/storage/states.tsv.gz` (about 9 KB, about 2,100 points) from that download; run it with the path of the zip and commit the result. Its steps are the same every time, so the output is reproducible:

- Only the lines between two states are kept: an edge of a state’s outline that another state’s outline also has. The 50 states and the District of Columbia border each other this way; Puerto Rico has no neighbour, so it has no line.
- The coast, the Great Lakes shores and the borders with Canada and Mexico are left out. The map’s land layer already draws them, and a second, differently simplified copy of the same shore would sit a little off the first.
- The edges between each pair of states are joined into lines that run from tripoint to tripoint (or to the coast), then each is simplified to within 0.012 degrees (about a kilometre) and its coordinates are rounded to four decimals, written as the first point and the steps from each point to the next.

The file has one row per line: the two states’ postal codes and its points. The app loads it once, the first time a trip’s map needs it, and keeps it in memory. What the trip map receives is only coordinates: the pieces of those lines inside the area the replay map can show for that trip (the box around all its places, flights and drives, widened to what the map shows around it, so the borders under a long flight are included), at most 1,500 steps (when more would be needed, the points are thinned, never a whole line dropped) and rounded to about a hundred metres, never a state’s name, anything about who travels or what was booked. A trip with no place in the United States gets none. The lines are coarse: a state line looks right at the replay’s zoom but is not a survey, and a river border can be off by a mile or so.
